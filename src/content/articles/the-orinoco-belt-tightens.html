Disclaimer

The folks at Carbon Sense are not making predictions about what will happen in Venezuela, nor arguments about what should. We are simply running the numbers.

Venezuela’s oil reserves are a national asset, and it is entirely legitimate for Venezuelans to seek economic recovery after years of hardship. This analysis does not argue that Venezuela should be treated differently from other resource-rich countries or denied development opportunities.

Instead, we use scenarios to explore what could happen under different investment pathways and what those pathways imply for emissions and global warming. The goal is not advocacy, but clarity: understanding how capital investment, production economics, and climate constraints interact once oil infrastructure is built.

Introduction

On the night of Friday 2nd of January, the United States stunned the world by capturing Nicolás Maduro, the sitting president of Venezuela, in a raid in Caracas. We will leave the ramifications on geopolitics and international law to others that understand far more than us at Carbon Sense.

However, a central part of the United States’ motivation for removing Maduro is Venezuela’s oil reserves. Data say they are the largest of any country on the planet and Donald Trump has urged oil companies to invest $100bn. While this has also been widely commented on, in this article we focus on a less discussed reverberation: what impact could revived Venezuelan oil production have on climate change?

To answer this question, first we consider Venezuela’s reserves and historic production, before mapping out potential future production scenarios spanning from a continuation of the status quo to reaching full potential.

Venezuela has vast reserves but produces very little

While the Middle East often comes to mind when thinking of oil stocks, it is actually Venezuela that has the largest proved oil reserves. In principle, ‘proved’ oil reserves are those that can most likely be extracted under current economic and technical conditions.[1]

The chart below, based on data from the Energy Institutes’ 2025 Statistical Review of World Energy, shows the ten countries with the largest provided oil reserves. Venezuela has more oil than Saudi Arabia and more than Iran and Iraq combined. These reserves account for 18% of the global total.

Venezuela did not always hold this mantle. Between 2006 and 2013, parts of its Orinoco Belt were reclassified as proved reserves. These reserves are “heavy” or “extra heavy”, which has two important implications.

First, such crude is significantly more expensive to extract and process than conventional oil. Its dense, tar-like consistency means it does not flow naturally and often requires dilution, upgrading, or energy-intensive recovery techniques. As a result, production from the Orinoco Belt is highly dependent on oil prices and investment conditions: when prices are low or capital is scarce, projects quickly become uneconomic.

Here, it is worth dwelling on what ‘proved’ oil reserves really are. In principle, as mentioned above, they are deposits that can most likely be extracted under current economic and technical conditions.[2] As the crude in the Orinoco Belt can quickly become uneconomic under certain conditions, some analysts argue that the share of Venezuela’s ‘proved’ reserves that is truly recoverable may be smaller than headline figures suggest. That said, even if this is true, Venezuela would continue to hold substantial reserves.

Second, heavy and extra heavy crude require significantly more energy to extract, transport, and refine into usable fuels. This makes Venezuela’s oil among the most carbon-intensive in the world.[3] According to industry estimates from S&P Global Platts Analytics, upstream emissions from Orinoco Belt crude are around 1,460 kg CO₂e per barrel of oil equivalent. This is almost 1,000 times higher than the world’s least carbon intensive reserves, such as Norway’s Johan Sverdrup field (about 1.6 kg CO₂e per barrel of oil equivalent).

Despite its reserves, Venezuela produces relatively little compared to other oil-rich countries. In 2020, Venezuela produced only around 5% as much oil as the United States, as well substantially less than other countries with smaller proved reserves. This production accounts for just 1% of the global total.

Combining proved reserves and current production gives the ‘reserves-to-production ratio’ (R/P ratio). In simple terms, it measures how many years a country’s proved oil reserves would last if production stayed constant and no additional reserves became economically viable. Those are, of course, very strong assumptions, but the results are revealing nonetheless.[4]

Venezuela’s R/P ratio is exceptionally large because it combines an enormous proved reserves with low production. In 2020, a trough for Venezuelan output, Energy Institute data suggest an R/P ratio well over 1,000 years.[5]

This stands in sharp contrast to countries like the United States, whose R/P ratio is much lower, around a decade, not because it is about to exhaust its oil resources, but because it produces large volumes continuously while replenishing proved reserves through ongoing exploration, investment, and technological change.

Venezuela’s oil production since the 1990s has not been constrained by geology as much as it has by changing politics.

  • During the final years of the Punto Fijo era in the 1990s, Venezuelan oil production increased year-on-year to a peak of 3.5 million barrels per day (in blue on the chart below). During this period, the state oil company PDVSA and private operators had relatively strong technical capacity and infrastructure in place, supported by foreign investment.
  • During Hugo Chávez’s presidency, from 1999 to 2013, oil production remained over 2.7 million barrels per day. Chávez’s policies nationalised large parts of the oil sector, transferring control of major projects from foreign operators to the state and political appointees, while investment in maintenance and exploration increasingly fell behind. Several of these transfers later became the subject of international arbitration claims by United States and European oil companies, many of which were upheld.[6] This legacy of legal uncertainty continues to weigh on investor confidence and on the willingness of international firms to return.
  • Under Nicolás Maduro, oil production collapsed to only 0.7 million barrels per day in 2020, before recovering slightly. Deep underinvestment and maintenance neglect weakened infrastructure further, while United States sanctions restricted the export of oil. The heaviness of Venezuela’s crude oil, noted above, also helps explain this decline: unlike lighter grades, production requires continuous investment in infrastructure; without it, production declines are steeper and recovery more costly.

So what does this all mean? Is Venezuela sitting on a ticking time bomb?

Of course, it is important to stress that what happens next in Venezuela’s oil sector is uncertain. Production outcomes will depend on a complex mix of factors. So rather than offer a forecast, we set out four illustrative scenarios intended to capture potential outcomes.

Our approach in these scenarios is deliberately simplified. We anchor production at today’s levels and then specify alternative paths for future evolution.[7] In each case, we assume that any increase in Venezuelan production above today’s level translates into additional oil ultimately consumed globally, and estimate the associated emissions and warming impact.[8]

These are strong assumptions but they serve a purpose. They allow us to isolate the potential climate consequence of expanding production in Venezuela, without layering in the speculative dynamics like oil prices, OPEC behaviour, or displacement of other producers.

Taken together, these scenarios span outcomes that range from the status quo to reaching full potential. For those interested, the annex gives production paths and subsequent calculations.

Scenario 0: Status Quo

This is the world we already know. Investment remains scarce, production limps along at roughly today’s level, and Venezuela’s vast reserves stay largely untouched. In fact, some reporting suggests that Venezuelan production may fall below current levels in the near term. Factors such as continued export restrictions, low international investor confidence following previous transfers of control, and shortages of naphtha used to dilute heavy crude could push output lower absent political resolution.[9]

From a climate perspective, this is the least dramatic outcome. Emissions are real, but modest relative to proved reserves. Most of the oil remains underground, not because of deliberate climate restraint, but because the investment and political conditions required to extract it simply are not in place.

Averaged over the period from now to 2050, this level of production translates into around 0.9 GtCO₂ per year in emissions — roughly equivalent to co-author Andrew flying home from Europe to New Zealand 430 million times a year. Using standard IPCC estimates, that equates to 0.01°C of warming over the period.

That is not nothing. But relative to Venezuela’s geological endowment, it is barely a rounding error. In this scenario, most of the country’s oil, and potential emissions, remains underground. The climate outcome here is shaped less by policy choice than by circumstance: scarce investment, degraded infrastructure, and a political environment that has kept production stuck well below its historical peak.

Scenario 1: Modest But Sustained Recovery

This represents a cautious recovery, based on analysis from ClimatePartner. In this world, investment returns slowly. Production rises by around 0.5 million barrels per day by 2028, before reaching roughly 1.6 million barrels per day above today’s level by the mid-2030s, where it then stabilises.

This path still falls well short of Venezuela’s historical production peak in the 1990s but marks a clear break from stagnation. It is consistent with a sanctions relief, renewed access to diluents, selective foreign investment, and incremental repairs to existing infrastructure — without assuming a wholesale transformation of the sector or a surge to historic highs.

Averaged over the period to 2050, this scenario means an additional 0.9 GtCO₂ per year compared to Scenario 0. Put differently, in total now Andrew is now flying home about 860 million times a year. Cumulatively, and again using standard IPCC estimates, this equates to a further 0.01°C of warming between now and 2050.

Despite its modest scale, this scenario would absorb 13% of the remaining carbon budget consistent with limiting warming to 1.5°C.[10] The lesson is subtle but important: even a relatively restrained recovery, if sustained over decades, can carry significant climate consequences in a world with very little remaining headroom.

Scenario 2: Significant Recovery

In this world, capital returns more decisively and institutional conditions improve enough to support large-scale reinvestment. Infrastructure is repaired, skilled labour returns, and production steadily recovers. By 2035, output rises from today’s roughly 1.1 million barrels per day back toward Venezuela’s historical peak of around 3.4 million barrels per day.[11]

Averaged over the period to 2050, this scenario adds around 1.3 GtCO₂ per year compared to Scenario 0 - equivalent to Andrew making the journey home about 1.47 billion. Cumulatively, this translates into roughly 0.015°C of additional warming between now and 2050 and would consume around 19% of the remaining carbon budget consistent with the 1.5°C target.

This is the point where sunk capital begins to dominate the story. Once wells are drilled and pipelines laid, fixed costs are already paid and marginal extraction costs fall sharply. Each additional barrel becomes relatively inexpensive to produce even as its climate costs remain unchanged. At that stage, responding to climate signals becomes more difficult. This lock-in effect means that, absent strong carbon pricing or binding constraints, once capital is committed, production persists.

This scenario closely resembles the revival implied by United States political narratives that emphasise restoring Venezuela’s oil industry to its former strength.

Scenario 3: Reaching Production Capacity

Saudi Arabia currently produces around 10 million barrels of oil per day, a scale that is unrealistic for Venezuela given the nature of its crude and logistical constraints. Even so, some reporting suggests that Venezuela’s potential production capacity could eventually reach 6 million barrels per day.[12]

In climate terms, this is where the numbers really bite. Averaged over the period to 2050, this scenario adds around 1.9 GtCO₂ per year compared to Scenario 0. Andrew is now home 2.37 billion times a year. Cumulatively, this equates to a further 0.021°C of warming between now and 2050.

While that temperature increase may still appear small in isolation, the budgetary impact is not. This pathway could consume 28% of the remaining carbon budget consistent with 1.5°C. In other words, under this scenario, a single country’s oil expansion would claim more than a quarter of the world’s remaining margin for error.

Conclusion — The Trump Factor

Taken in isolation, the temperature impacts associated with expanded Venezuelan oil production appear modest when expressed in fractions of a degree. But this framing risks missing the more important point. The remaining carbon budget consistent with limiting warming to 1.5 °C is now extremely small, leaving little room for error. Under some assumptions, a modest but sustained recovery in production could alone absorb around 13% of that remaining budget. Under more ambitious, though less likely, production pathways implied by other reporting, that share could rise toward a quarter or more of the total.

The precise outcome is deeply uncertain, shaped by a wide range of factors. Most analysts expect only a limited recovery in the near term, but uncertainty cuts both ways: once capital is committed and infrastructure rebuilt, production becomes harder to reverse, and the climate consequences accumulate.

In a world already perilously close to the 1.5 °C threshold, decisions taken today, particularly by the United States, about Venezuela’s oil sector are no longer geopolitical or economic choices; they are climate choices. The margin for error is thin, and policy now sits uncomfortably close to a cliff edge.

Annex: Production pathways and climate calculations

This annex explains how the production pathways used in each scenario are translated into estimates of carbon dioxide emissions, global warming, and shares of the carbon budget.

The chart below summarises the four production pathways; the main text sets out the reasoning for each. These annual production profiles are the starting point for all subsequent calculations.

Production pathways in each Scenario


From production pathways to total output

For each year in each scenario, daily production (in million barrels per day) is converted into annual oil production by multiplying by 365 days and by one million. Adding these annual figures across the period from 2025 to 2050 gives total oil production over the horizon.

In the article, we also distinguish between total production and additional production. Additional production is defined as production above the status quo level of 1.1 million barrels per day. This allows us to isolate the climate impact of expanding Venezuelan production relative to today’s baseline.

From production to cumulative CO2 emissions

Oil production is converted into carbon dioxide emissions using a constant lifecycle emissions factor of 1.89 tonnes of CO₂ per barrel, applied uniformly across all scenarios. This factor captures both the emissions released when oil is ultimately burned and the additional emissions associated with extraction, upgrading, transport, and refining — which are particularly significant for Venezuela’s heavy crude.

Applying the same emissions factor in each case ensures that differences across scenarios are driven solely by production volumes, rather than by assumptions about technology, efficiency, or fuel composition. For each scenario, we calculate cumulative emissions over the period from 2025 to 2050, as well as the average annual emissions over that horizon. The scenario descriptions in the main text report these average annual values, expressed in gigatonnes of CO₂ per year.

From cumulative emissions to temperature increase

To estimate the warming impact, we rely on the well-established near-linear relationship between cumulative CO₂ emissions and global mean temperature rise. This relationship is known as the Transient Climate Response to Cumulative Emissions of CO₂ (TCRE). This relationship implies that each additional tonne of CO₂ emissions contributes a roughly constant increment to global warming, around 4.5 × 10⁻¹³ °C per tonne of CO₂, or 0.00045 °C per gigatonne, largely independent of when those emissions occur. This relationship underpins the concept of a global carbon budget and is widely used in IPCC assessments.

When the article refers to temperature increases such as “0.01°C between now and 2050”, this reflects the cumulative emissions associated with the production pathways shown in above, translated using this standard relationship.

Carbon budget shares

We also express cumulative emissions from each scenario as a share of the remaining global carbon budget consistent with limiting warming to 1.5°C. This is done by comparing total emissions from the scenario with an estimate of the remaining budget of around 170 gigatonnes of CO₂.
